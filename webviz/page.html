Mouse vs. Hominid

Can computer vision objectively determine, whether mice are too different from hominids and thus disqualified as model organism?

Photos of the drawings were cropped to their ink content, converted to grayscale, and cached as 160×160 PNGs (resized to 128×128 at load time) so training doesn't re-decode full-resolution source JPEGs every epoch. Training augmentation used random rotation, horizontal flip, small translation, and scale jitter; validation images are unaugmented.

The classifier fine-tunes a pretrained EfficientNet-B0 backbone (ImageNet weights). With only 125 training images, fine-tuning the entire 4M-parameter backbone would just memorize the training set, so everything except the final MBConv block and the classifier head is frozen — 1.7M of the 4.0M total parameters are actually trained.

04  Breaking it — blackout occlusion

A more literal test than GradCAM: physically paint a black square over part of the drawing and re-run the classifier. Does the model change its mind? To keep the occlusion honest, the search is now capped at a 32px square (about a quarter of the 128px canvas) instead of letting it grow until something flips, and it's run over every image in the dataset rather than stopping once 10 flips are found — so the numbers below are the true flip rate, not a cherry-picked set of successes.

58 / 80mouse flipped (72.5%) at ≤ 32px
3 / 77hominid flipped (3.9%) at ≤ 32px

Mouse → 58/80 flipped at ≤ 32px (72.5%)

Hominid → 3/77 flipped at ≤ 32px (3.9%)

Capped at a modest 32px square and run over the full dataset, the two classes turn out to have very different robustness: 72.5% of mouse drawings (58/80) can be flipped to "hominid" with a single small black square, but only 3.9% of hominid drawings (3/77) can be flipped to "mouse" the same way. This can be interpreted as that if we squint our eyes, or if an inconvenient black square is in our visual field, we can see mice as hominids. Not the other way though. One conclusion from this can be that mice are actually a valid model for studying humans. With the caveat that humans are not great models for studying mice.

05  Synthesizing a class from nothing

GradCAM and occlusion both show where in a real drawing the model is looking — but not what shape it actually learned. To test that directly: start from a blank canvas (no dataset photo involved at all) and run gradient ascent on the classifier's own output logit, synthesizing whatever image most convinces it that it's looking at a "mouse" or a "hominid." Ink is parameterized as a small number of continuous Bezier strokes with a fixed pen width (not a pixel grid), so the model can only spend its ink budget by drawing actual lines, never by scattering points. Rather than matching the real dataset's total ink coverage (a scalar budget), the loss now matches the radially-averaged spatial-frequency spectrum of real drawings — rewarding the same balance of fine-line-scale vs. blob-scale structure that real hand-drawn ink has, not just the same amount of it. Swapping the ink-budget penalty for a spatial-frequency match changes the character of the result more than the headline "hominid vs. mouse" shape story. Both classes now converge to wobbly, scalloped lines with periodic fine-scale texture along their length, rather than the smooth single strokes the ink-matched version found — because matching a real drawing's frequency spectrum rewards exactly that kind of fine-grained irregularity, not just a thin continuous curve. Within that shared texture, the classes still diverge in overall structure: hominid syntheses tend toward one or two loosely closed, loopy forms — a rough torso-and-limb silhouette rather than a single clean line — while mouse syntheses are consistently busier and more tangled, with several strokes crossing and knotting near the center of the canvas. The frequency prior naturally settles on more total ink than the real dataset average (roughly 6–8% coverage here vs. ~1–2% in the real photos) since matching spectral shape doesn't directly cap total ink the way the old budget penalty did — but the class-conditional shape difference survives the change in prior, which is some evidence it isn't an artifact of the ink-matching setup specifically.

06  Conclusion

Three independent probes — GradCAM, occlusion, and de-novo synthesis — end up telling a consistent story about what each class actually looks like to this model. Looking across all three: hominid seems to be defined mainly by the core/crotch region, with a limb reaching off to one side or another — a reading that matches GradCAM's activations clustering around the torso, occlusion's finding that hominid evidence is diffuse enough that no single small patch reliably erases it, and synthesis converging on one or two loosely closed, torso-and-limb-like loops. Mouse reads as defined by a pair of prominent ears together with a rounded back held close by — consistent with GradCAM's more localized hotspots, occlusion's finding that a single small patch flips most mice, and synthesis landing on busier, tangled marks clustered tightly rather than spread across the canvas. More broadly, humans read as "limby" to this network — an extended form built from a core plus reaching limbs — while mice read as a compact aggregation of bigger, rounder features held close together. The asymmetry that runs through all three tests — hominid as one diffuse, holistic form vs. mouse as a couple of compact, swappable features in close proximity — is a plausible reason the model finds mouse so much easier to disrupt with a single occlusion, and hominid so much harder.

That occlusion asymmetry only runs one way: squint, or drop one inconvenient black square in the wrong place, and a mouse can start to look like a hominid to this model — but the reverse essentially never happens (only 3.9% of hominids flip, vs. 72.5% of mice). That's almost a computational vindication of the mouse as a model organism for humans — a mouse's compact feature set can degrade into "human enough," but a human's more diffuse, spread-out structure doesn't degrade into "mouse enough" nearly as easily. The reverse claim, that humans make a good model for studying mice, gets no support here at all.

We work with mice to study vision and wish to extrapolate our findings to humans. But mice are nocturnal animals adapted to whisk, smell and hear their way through the world. Such is the valid criticism of this model.

As a mouse researcher I am biased. That is why I reached out to computer vision to provide the ultimate objective truth.
