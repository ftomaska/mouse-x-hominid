Can computer vision objectively determine, whether mice are too different from hominids and thus disqualified as model organism?

Photos of the drawings were cropped to their ink content, converted to grayscale, and cached as 160×160 PNGs (resized to 128×128 at load time) so training doesn't re-decode full-resolution source JPEGs every epoch. Training augmentation used random rotation, horizontal flip, small translation, and scale jitter; validation images are unaugmented.

The classifier fine-tunes a pretrained EfficientNet-B0 backbone (ImageNet weights). With only 125 training images, fine-tuning the entire 4M-parameter backbone would just memorize the training set, so everything except the final MBConv block and the classifier head is frozen — 1.7M of the 4.0M total parameters are actually trained.

Optimized with AdamW (lr 3e-4, weight decay 1e-4) and cosine annealing over 25 epochs, cross-entropy loss, batch size 16, ImageNet normalization. Validation accuracy reached 100% by epoch 18 and briefly held it before mild overfitting set back in as the frozen backbone's few trainable layers kept fitting a training set this small — the checkpoint saved is the epoch-18 best, not the final epoch:

04  Breaking it — blackout occlusion

One interpretation of the existence of a somewhat consistent GradCAM heatmap is that there is a body part or feature the model finds most important for classifying. What would happen if we occluded a little part of the image and re-run the classifier? Does it truly depend on a small detail? Can a mouse be seen as a hominid? Can a hominid be seen as a mouse?

We limited the occlusion to max 32px (of the 128px canvas) and counted how many samples of the whole dataset we were able to 'flip' to the opposite class.

58 / 80mouse flipped (72.5%) at ≤ 32px
3 / 77hominid flipped (3.9%) at ≤ 32px

Mouse → 58/80 flipped at ≤ 32px (72.5%)

Hominid → 3/77 flipped at ≤ 32px (3.9%)

Our two classes seem to have very different robustness to this blackout occlusion test. 72.5% of mouse drawings (58/80) can be flipped to "hominid" with a single small black square, but only 3.9% of hominid drawings (3/77) can be flipped to "mouse" the same way.

Could this mean that if we squint our eyes, metaphorically or literally or if something occludes are vision, we can see mice as hominids? Moreover, this does not work the other way!

One conclusion from this can be that mice are actually a valid model for studying humans. With the caveat that humans are not great models for studying mice. The model is objective after all.

06  Conclusion

We took a model that has seen the world/the ImageNet database, and showed it hominids and mice. We saw where the model looks for information when having to decide, we found that it is easy to fool into thinking a mouse is a hominid, but not the other way and finally we saw its somewhat abstract ideas of our shapes.

Hominids are "limby" crotch-centric figures, while mice are defined by their ears with a round back in close proximity. And yet by squinting its eyes, or having carefully placing a small black square, this objective judge can mistake a mouse for a hominid. This can be interpreted as a computational confirmation that mice are valid model organisms for humans. Surprisingly, though, we should be careful when extrapolating ideas from humans to mice as it might not be as easy.

We work with mice to study vision and wish to extrapolate our findings to humans. But mice are nocturnal animals adapted to whisk, smell and hear their way through the world. Such is the valid criticism of this model.

As a mouse researcher I am biased. That is why I reached out to computer vision to provide the ultimate objective truth.
